Kirk Case Studies

Claims about the killing of Charlie Kirk on 10 September 2025 are made from video. This site tests them at the level the video allows: frame by frame, with the timing checked, the original pixels preserved and the limits of each copy stated. Each study keeps its question, finding and limitations together.

What the footage can and cannot show

Each study starts from a specific claim someone has made about the video, states what observation would strengthen or weaken it, and then looks. The numbers identify the studies; they do not rank their importance. Earlier versions remain part of the record.

01 · Frame-level video analysis · three cameras, one original · V3

Two gestures before the shot

Did two men on the security line press something in the seconds before the shot, and try to hide it?

Finding / scope
Zero is the first frame in which Kirk's head and shoulders move abruptly, tied across the three clips by their shared sound to 0.05 s. The plaid-shirt man's right hand leaves the barrier rail 0.3 s before that frame and his index finger is on his left sleeve 0.2 s before it; no button, strap or device resolves at 1080p. The blue-shirt man's arms stay folded through it with the right hand hidden, the pocket unchanged for 0.2 s after; the right fist is out 0.47 s after. The man first taken for the sleeve-scratcher, in a white polo, is holding a phone. Every step is shown at the frame, with close-up loops.
Limitations
A hand out of view cannot be shown still. The anchor is a human reading of one frame per clip, checked by audio alignment to about 0.05 s. The plaid-shirt man is seen from one camera. No blinded rating was run; purpose is not addressed. V1 anchored on the wrong sound and reversed the plaid-shirt finding; V2 marked Kirk's later snap; both corrections are on the page.

A claim is written down first, with the observation that would move it. The inputs are recorded with their provenance and checksums. Every figure is produced by a script in the repository, and the page reports the finding with its limitations at the same size.
Rules

How the studies are run

A video does not show an event. It shows a sequence of frames that a camera, then several pieces of software, made of the event. The studies treat every clip that way.

  1. Originals over copies. Timing claims are made from camera originals wherever one exists. Re-encoded, screen-recorded or edited copies are used for what they can still show, with their frame-rate, duplicates and audio alignment checked first. This follows the frame-timing guidance of the Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence.
  2. Frames are the smallest unit. At 30 frames per second successive frames are about 33 ms apart, at 60 frames per second about 17 ms. Slow playback adds nothing between them. Every time on these pages is a frame's presentation timestamp from the file, not a count divided by a nominal rate.
  3. Sound is not the shot. The loud transient in a clip is the arrival of sound at that camera. A supersonic bullet's crack and the muzzle report arrive at different times, and each copy may carry its own audio offset. The pages report the transient and Kirk's first visible movement separately.
  4. Three levels of claim. An observable movement (a hand contacts an arm), an identifiable action (the frames resolve the operation of an object) and a purpose (concealment, communication, participation) are graded separately. Footage rarely reaches the second level and never the third on its own.
  5. Earlier behaviour is the comparison. A movement close to the shot is weighed against how often the same person makes comparable movements in the minutes before it, using the same measurement.
  6. Living persons. The people in the frames are described by clothing and position only. Nothing on these pages identifies them or imputes intent, and the finding of a study is about what the frames support, never about anyone's guilt.
Reproducibility

Every figure is made by pipeline/s1_video.py from the input files listed with their checksums in inputs/. Tracking coordinates, per-frame motion series and timing tables are versioned in data/. Nothing on the pages was drawn by hand.

Material

Where the clips come from

The studies work from a public collection of event footage on the Internet Archive, archive.org/download/kirkshooting, which gathers phone clips that circulated after the shooting. The collection names files by number. Each study lists the exact files it used, their upload dates, sizes and SHA-256 checksums, and what the container metadata says about how each copy was made. One file, 2.MOV, is an iPhone camera original with its recording time and location still in the container; the others are copies of varying quality.

The collection is not a chain of custody. Where a study needs a native file that is not in it, the study says so.
